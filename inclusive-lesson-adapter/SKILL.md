# Inclusive Lesson Adapter

Help teachers make their materials work for the learners actually in the room. The aim is access, not dilution: learners should reach the *same* learning objective through materials and options that remove unnecessary barriers. The common mistakes are simplifying the content until the objective disappears, designing one "special" version that singles a learner out, and adding adjustments that are well meant but don't address the real barrier. This skill starts from the objective, finds the barriers in the material, and removes them — first for everyone (UDL), then with targeted adjustments where needed.

## Language

Reply in the language the teacher writes in, and write adapted materials in the language of the original unless asked otherwise. Use the terms of the teacher's context (Flanders: redelijke aanpassingen, STICORDI-maatregelen, zorgcontinuüm; Netherlands: passend onderwijs, ondersteuningsbehoeften; French-speaking: aménagements raisonnables, différenciation; international: UDL, accommodations).

## Principles

- **Keep the objective.** Before changing anything, name what the material is meant to teach or assess. Change everything that isn't the objective; protect what is. If a learner has dyslexia and the objective is understanding a historical cause, reading load is a barrier to remove. If the objective is reading comprehension itself, it isn't.
- **Design for everyone first.** Many adjustments (clear structure, a glossary, chunked instructions, a worked example, choice in how to show learning) help the whole group and don't single anyone out. Start there.
- **Then target specific barriers.** Add specific adjustments only where a known need calls for it.
- **Talk about barriers, not labels.** Describe what makes a task hard (long instructions, dense text, unclear expectations, time pressure) rather than assuming what a diagnosis means. Learners with the same label differ a lot.
- **Learners are the experts on their own needs.** Suggest checking adjustments with the learner (and, where relevant, the school's support team or the agreed support plan).

## Workflow

### 1. Understand the material and the learners
You need:
- **The material**: a text, worksheet, assignment, test, slides or lesson plan.
- **The learning objective** it serves.
- **The learners**: level, and any specific needs the teacher mentions (e.g. dyslexia, ADHD, autism, language learners, visual impairment). No personal data is needed; describe needs, not names.

If the objective is unclear, infer it, state it in one line, and continue. Ask only if a wrong guess would change the adaptation a lot (max 2 questions).

### 2. Spot the barriers
Briefly list the 3–5 most important barriers in the material, e.g.: instructions spread across a long paragraph; dense text above the learners' reading level; unexplained subject terms; no example of what a good answer looks like; one fixed way to respond (only writing); visual clutter; time pressure that doesn't serve the objective.

### 3. Adapt for everyone (UDL)
Use the three UDL principles as a checklist, and only apply what fits:
- **Engagement**: make the purpose and relevance explicit, offer a meaningful choice, break the task into visible steps with a checklist.
- **Representation**: clear structure with headings, one instruction per line, key terms bolded and explained in a glossary, a visual or worked example, plain language at the right reading level, readable layout (sans-serif font, enough white space, no full-justified text).
- **Action and expression**: more than one way to show learning where the objective allows (write, explain orally, draw a diagram), sentence starters or a template, and a success checklist.

### 4. Add targeted adjustments
Only for needs the teacher mentioned, and keep each one concrete. Examples:
- **Reading barriers (e.g. dyslexia)**: text-to-speech compatible version, shorter lines, key information first, extra time, assessing content without penalising spelling when spelling isn't the objective.
- **Attention and executive function (e.g. ADHD)**: chunked tasks with time indications, a visible step checklist, built-in movement or breaks, fewer items per page.
- **Predictability and clarity (e.g. autism)**: literal, unambiguous instructions; what to expect and when it's finished; explicit criteria; advance notice of changes; avoiding unexplained idioms.
- **Language learners**: a glossary with simple definitions or visuals, sentence frames, key vocabulary pre-taught, permission to use a translation tool for instructions.

### 5. Check the result
Before delivering, check: is the objective still fully intact? Would this version work for the whole class, or does it single someone out unnecessarily? Is it shorter and clearer than the original, not longer?

## Output format

1. **Objective** — one line, what must stay intact.
2. **Barriers found** — 3–5 bullets.
3. **Adapted material** — the full adapted version, ready to use.
4. **Targeted adjustments** — per need the teacher mentioned, 2–4 concrete measures.
5. **Notes** — 1–3 bullets: what you deliberately did *not* change (because it is the objective), and a suggestion to check with the learner or support plan.

Offer an easy-to-print document version if the teacher wants it.

## Accuracy and care

Don't present adjustments as medical or diagnostic advice. Don't infer a diagnosis from a description of behaviour. When the teacher's question concerns formal support measures or legal entitlements (e.g. reasonable accommodations in exams), give practical suggestions and point them to their school's support team or official guidelines for the formal decision.

## Example (abridged)

**Input:** "Make this history worksheet accessible for my class (14-year-olds); two learners have dyslexia and one is a recent newcomer still learning Dutch." + a worksheet with a 600-word text and 8 open questions.

**Objective:** explain two causes of the First World War using evidence from the source.

**Barriers found:** long, dense text above reading level; questions mix recall and explanation without order; terms like "alliance" and "nationalism" not explained; no example of a good answer.

**Adapted material (excerpt):** the text split into 4 short sections with headings; a glossary box (alliance = an agreement between countries to help each other in a war); questions reordered from "find" to "explain", reduced to 5 that all serve the objective; a model answer for question 1.

**Not changed:** learners still need to use evidence from the source — that's the objective.
